For most of human history, the question of where a ship was on the open sea had no precise answer. Sailors who stayed within sight of land could steer by headlands, river mouths and familiar hills, but once the coast dropped below the horizon they had to rely on a mixture of observation, memory and educated guesswork. The story of how navigators learned to fix their position far from land is a story about the sky, about clocks, and about the slow accumulation of knowledge by people who rarely wrote down what they knew.

The earliest long-distance sailors read the sea itself. Polynesian voyagers crossed thousands of kilometres of the Pacific Ocean in double-hulled canoes, settling islands as distant from each other as Hawaii, New Zealand and Easter Island. They had no instruments in the modern sense. Instead, a navigator memorised the rising and setting points of dozens of stars, and used them as a compass that turned through the night. He felt the long swells that rolled across the ocean from distant storms, and could tell from the way they crossed one another that an island lay ahead, bending the waves around it. He watched the flight of birds that slept on land and fished at sea, and noticed the colour of clouds that hung over lagoons. This knowledge was passed from teacher to student over many years, and much of it was lost when the voyaging traditions declined.

In the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean, sailors developed other methods. Arab and Persian navigators measured the height of the Pole Star above the horizon using a simple device made of a wooden board and a knotted string. The higher the star appeared, the further north the ship had travelled. Because the Pole Star sits almost directly above the Earth's axis, its height in the sky is close to the observer's latitude, the angular distance north of the equator. A sailor who knew the latitude of his destination could sail north or south until the star stood at the right height, and then turn east or west and follow that line of latitude until land appeared. This technique, sometimes called running down the latitude, remained in use for centuries because it was simple and reliable.

The magnetic compass arrived in European waters during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, probably after being used earlier in China. At first it was a magnetised needle floating on a piece of cork in a bowl of water; later it was mounted on a pivot beneath a card marked with the points of the wind. The compass did not tell a navigator where he was, but it told him which way he was heading even when clouds hid the sun and the stars. Combined with an estimate of speed and the time elapsed, it allowed a method known as dead reckoning: starting from a known position, the navigator plotted each leg of the voyage on a chart, adding up distances and directions to estimate where the ship must now be. Speed was measured by throwing overboard a piece of wood attached to a line with knots tied at regular intervals, and counting how many knots ran out while a small sandglass emptied. This is why a ship's speed is still given in knots.

Dead reckoning had an obvious weakness. Every small error in the estimated speed, the steered course or the effect of currents added to the next, and over a long voyage the errors grew until the plotted position could be wrong by hundreds of kilometres. Navigators corrected their latitude whenever they could observe the sun at noon or a known star at night. For this they used instruments that grew steadily more precise: the mariner's astrolabe, a heavy brass ring that hung from the hand; the cross-staff, a wooden rod with a sliding crosspiece; and later the backstaff, which allowed the observer to stand with his back to the sun and measure its height by the shadow it cast. Each improvement reduced the error in latitude, but none of them solved the harder problem.

That harder problem was longitude, the angular distance east or west of a chosen reference meridian. Latitude can be read from the sky because the heights of the sun and stars change as one moves north or south. Longitude cannot be read in the same way, because the Earth turns, and the sky over a ship in the Atlantic looks the same as the sky over a ship further west, only a few hours later. The difference in longitude between two places is therefore a difference in time. Since the Earth turns through fifteen degrees every hour, a navigator who knew the exact time at his home port, and could compare it with the local time on board, could calculate how far east or west he had sailed. Local time was easy to find by watching for the moment when the sun reached its highest point. The difficulty was knowing the time at home.
