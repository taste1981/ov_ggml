The history of the printing press begins long before Johannes Gutenberg built his famous machine in Mainz around the middle of the fifteenth century. In China, craftsmen carved whole pages of text into wooden blocks, covered the raised characters with ink, and pressed paper against them to make copies of Buddhist scriptures, calendars, and official notices. Later, a craftsman named Bi Sheng experimented with movable type made of baked clay, so that individual characters could be arranged, printed, and then reused for a different page. In Korea, printers cast movable type in bronze and produced books for the royal court and for scholars. Gutenberg combined several existing ideas into one practical system: metal type cast in an adjustable mould, an oil-based ink that stuck to metal, and a screw press adapted from the presses used to make wine and olive oil. His workshop could produce pages far faster than scribes copying by hand, and within fifty years printing shops had spread to more than two hundred cities across Europe. Books became cheaper, more people learned to read, and new ideas in science, religion, and politics travelled further and faster than ever before.
