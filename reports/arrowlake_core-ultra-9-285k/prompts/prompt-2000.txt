For most of human history, the question of where a ship was on the open sea had no precise answer. Sailors who stayed within sight of land could steer by headlands, river mouths and familiar hills, but once the coast dropped below the horizon they had to rely on a mixture of observation, memory and educated guesswork. The story of how navigators learned to fix their position far from land is a story about the sky, about clocks, and about the slow accumulation of knowledge by people who rarely wrote down what they knew.

The earliest long-distance sailors read the sea itself. Polynesian voyagers crossed thousands of kilometres of the Pacific Ocean in double-hulled canoes, settling islands as distant from each other as Hawaii, New Zealand and Easter Island. They had no instruments in the modern sense. Instead, a navigator memorised the rising and setting points of dozens of stars, and used them as a compass that turned through the night. He felt the long swells that rolled across the ocean from distant storms, and could tell from the way they crossed one another that an island lay ahead, bending the waves around it. He watched the flight of birds that slept on land and fished at sea, and noticed the colour of clouds that hung over lagoons. This knowledge was passed from teacher to student over many years, and much of it was lost when the voyaging traditions declined.

In the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean, sailors developed other methods. Arab and Persian navigators measured the height of the Pole Star above the horizon using a simple device made of a wooden board and a knotted string. The higher the star appeared, the further north the ship had travelled. Because the Pole Star sits almost directly above the Earth's axis, its height in the sky is close to the observer's latitude, the angular distance north of the equator. A sailor who knew the latitude of his destination could sail north or south until the star stood at the right height, and then turn east or west and follow that line of latitude until land appeared. This technique, sometimes called running down the latitude, remained in use for centuries because it was simple and reliable.

The magnetic compass arrived in European waters during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, probably after being used earlier in China. At first it was a magnetised needle floating on a piece of cork in a bowl of water; later it was mounted on a pivot beneath a card marked with the points of the wind. The compass did not tell a navigator where he was, but it told him which way he was heading even when clouds hid the sun and the stars. Combined with an estimate of speed and the time elapsed, it allowed a method known as dead reckoning: starting from a known position, the navigator plotted each leg of the voyage on a chart, adding up distances and directions to estimate where the ship must now be. Speed was measured by throwing overboard a piece of wood attached to a line with knots tied at regular intervals, and counting how many knots ran out while a small sandglass emptied. This is why a ship's speed is still given in knots.

Dead reckoning had an obvious weakness. Every small error in the estimated speed, the steered course or the effect of currents added to the next, and over a long voyage the errors grew until the plotted position could be wrong by hundreds of kilometres. Navigators corrected their latitude whenever they could observe the sun at noon or a known star at night. For this they used instruments that grew steadily more precise: the mariner's astrolabe, a heavy brass ring that hung from the hand; the cross-staff, a wooden rod with a sliding crosspiece; and later the backstaff, which allowed the observer to stand with his back to the sun and measure its height by the shadow it cast. Each improvement reduced the error in latitude, but none of them solved the harder problem.

That harder problem was longitude, the angular distance east or west of a chosen reference meridian. Latitude can be read from the sky because the heights of the sun and stars change as one moves north or south. Longitude cannot be read in the same way, because the Earth turns, and the sky over a ship in the Atlantic looks the same as the sky over a ship further west, only a few hours later. The difference in longitude between two places is therefore a difference in time. Since the Earth turns through fifteen degrees every hour, a navigator who knew the exact time at his home port, and could compare it with the local time on board, could calculate how far east or west he had sailed. Local time was easy to find by watching for the moment when the sun reached its highest point. The difficulty was knowing the time at home.

Mechanical clocks of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries could not keep time accurately at sea. Pendulums swung erratically as the ship rolled, changes in temperature made metal parts expand and contract, and dampness corroded the works. An error of only a few minutes in the clock meant an error of many kilometres in position. Ships ran aground on coasts that their navigators believed were still far away, and fleets were lost with all hands. In 1707 a squadron of the British navy struck the rocks of the Isles of Scilly in bad weather, and nearly two thousand sailors drowned. Disasters of this kind persuaded governments that the longitude problem was a matter of national importance.

In 1714 the British Parliament passed an act offering a large reward to anyone who could find longitude at sea to within half a degree. A board of commissioners was appointed to judge the proposals, and many of them were impractical or simply strange. Astronomers favoured a method based on the motion of the Moon, which moves against the background of the stars by about its own width every hour. If the position of the Moon relative to certain bright stars could be predicted years in advance and printed in tables, a navigator could measure the angle between the Moon and a star, look up the time at which the tables gave that angle, and so learn the time at the reference meridian. This lunar distance method was sound in principle, but the Moon's motion is complicated, and producing accurate tables took decades of observation and calculation.

Meanwhile, a carpenter and self-taught clockmaker named John Harrison set out to build a clock that could keep accurate time on a moving ship. His first marine timekeeper, completed in the 1730s, was a large and heavy machine with balanced arms that swung against each other so that the motion of the ship would cancel out. It performed well on a trial voyage, but Harrison was not satisfied and spent the next twenty years building two more large clocks, each more refined than the last. His fourth timekeeper took a completely different form. It looked like an oversized pocket watch, about thirteen centimetres across, and contained a fast-beating balance and a mechanism that compensated for changes in temperature. On a voyage to Jamaica in 1761 and 1762 it lost only a few seconds over more than two months at sea, an accuracy far beyond what the act required.

The commissioners were slow to accept that a watch could solve the problem. They demanded further trials, required Harrison to explain his methods and hand over his clocks, and asked other makers to reproduce his design to prove that it was not a lucky accident. Harrison, by then an old man, received most of the reward only after appealing to the king and to Parliament. Within a few decades, however, marine chronometers based on simpler versions of his ideas were being produced in workshops in London and Paris. They were expensive at first, and many ships continued to use lunar distances, which required only a sextant, a set of printed tables and a good deal of arithmetic. By the middle of the nineteenth century, though, chronometers had become cheap enough that most ocean-going vessels carried at least one, and often two or three so that their readings could be compared.

The sextant itself was another product of the eighteenth century. It used two mirrors to bring the image of the sun or a star down to the horizon, so that the observer could see both at once and measure the angle between them precisely, even on a rolling deck. With a sextant, a chronometer and a nautical almanac, a trained navigator could fix his position to within a few kilometres. The method of working out a position from these observations was refined during the nineteenth century, when navigators learned to treat each sight as a line on the chart along which the ship must lie. Where two such lines crossed, from two stars observed at dusk or dawn, was the ship's position.

The last great change came with radio. At the beginning of the twentieth century, stations began broadcasting time signals so that ships could check their chronometers against an accurate standard wherever they were. Later, networks of radio beacons allowed a ship to measure the direction or the timing of signals from transmitters on shore and calculate its position without seeing the sky at all. These systems were in turn replaced by satellite navigation, in which a receiver compares the timing of signals from several satellites and computes its position within a few metres. At the heart of every satellite is an atomic clock, and the whole system depends on keeping time to within billionths of a second. In that sense, modern navigation still rests on the insight that occupied Harrison and the astronomers of his day: to know where you are, you must know what time it is.
